Maui Travel Tips for the First-Time Visitor: 15 Things to Know

Nobody tells you that the highway speed limit drops to 25 mph through a dozen little beach towns, that your rental car should come with a windshield shade, or that the best snorkeling of your life may happen fifty feet from the condo lawn. Here are the 15 practical things experienced Maui visitors wish someone had told them before their first trip.

1. Decide which coast before you book anything

Maui is really two vacation destinations welded together. The south side around Kihei and Wailea is dry, sunny and reliably breezy, and the water tends to be calmer in the morning. West Maui around Kaanapali and Lahaina (still rebuilding after the 2023 fires) is greener and gets more frequent rain showers. If you plan to snorkel, swim and grill on a lanai most afternoons, South Maui is the easier base. Our own seventh-floor unit at 4711 S Kihei Rd sits right at the waterline, which shapes almost every recommendation below.

2. Book your rental car first, then your flights

This is the one logistics tip that saves real money. Maui's rental fleet is smaller than the visitor demand in peak season, and prices on OGG (Kahului Airport) jump well before flights do. Reserve at least three months out for winter, and book an intermediate or compact -- not a convertible. Convertibles are fun for a day and miserable after six hours of sun and windburn. Add the rental-car sunshade; you will thank yourself in the parking lot.

3. Budget a full day for arrival and a slower pace than you planned

Flights from the mainland typically land mid-afternoon, and by the time you shuttle to the car lot, stop for groceries and drive 25 minutes to Kihei, it is nearly dinner. Don't schedule a luau or a sunset sail on arrival day. First-timers consistently overpack their itinerary. Two activities per day is generous on Maui. Three is ambitious. Four means you spend your vacation in the car.

4. Learn the geography of one highway

Route 30 runs north from the airport through Kahului and Maalaea and down into Kihei, then continues up the west coast. Route 31 connects Kihei to Wailea and Makena via Piilani Highway. That is essentially the road network for your whole trip. Traffic on Route 30 between 7:30 and 9 a.m. and again from 3:30 to 5:30 p.m. is genuinely slow, and the northern stretch toward Kapalua is windy enough that passengers with motion sickness should look out the front window. Charge your phone mount -- there is no service on the backside of the island and spotty coverage in a few west Maui valleys.

5. Rent snorkel gear once, for the whole week

Weekly rentals in Kihei run roughly $20 to $35 for mask, snorkel and fins, which is less than two day trips with a tour boat. The reef quality off South Maui varies enormously by spot. If you want the full breakdown of conditions, read the best snorkeling spots near Kihei before you plan your mornings. And rinse the gear every evening -- saltwater corrodes buckles faster than you expect.

6. Snorkel early. Really early.

Wind picks up on the south shore almost every afternoon. If you are in the water by 7:30 a.m., you get glassy conditions, better visibility and fewer people. Our guests who snorkel off the lawn at unit 711 before breakfast reliably report seeing more than the ones who go out at four in the afternoon, and the Hawaiian green sea turtles tend to feed in the shallow reef edge in the morning anyway.

7. Do not touch the turtles. Do not stand on the coral.

Hawaii state law protects green sea turtles, and the voluntary viewing distance is about ten feet. It is illegal to approach, touch or chase them, and enforcement near popular beaches is real. Coral is equally fragile -- a single fin kick or a foot parked on a rock head can kill something that took decades to build. Float, don't stand, and stay off the reef's shallowest shelf.

8. Use reef-safe sunscreen and reapply it like a job

Hawaii bans the sale and distribution of sunscreens with oxybenzone and octinoxate. Mineral formulas with zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are the practical alternative, but they are also more prone to staining car upholstery and towels, so pack an extra beach towel. UV on the water is brutal because the ocean reflects it back at your face. Sunscreen every two hours, a wide-brim hat, and a rash guard will do more for you than any other single purchase.

9. Grocery shop the day you arrive, not day three

A week of dinners out on maui runs several hundred dollars a head. A week of breakfasts and a few grilled dinners at the condo runs less than $100 per person if you stock up smartly. Foodland, Costco and Times all have locations near Kahului. Our grocery shopping guide covers which stores are worth a stop and which to skip. Pick up coffee, fruit, eggs and frozen shrimp on your way in and you save your restaurant budget for two memorable dinners instead of seven mediocre ones.

10. Respect the ocean, and watch the lifeguard flags

Maui's surf can look gentle and then break hard over a shallow reef. Shorebreak at any beach in South Maui can dump you on the sand with real force. Never turn your back on the ocean while walking the shoreline. Read the posted signs. When lifeguards fly a red flag, it means stay out, no matter how many people you see in the water. Rough-water days are excellent days for the road to Hana instead.

11. Book your one splurge and your one sunrise ahead of time

Haleakala sunrise requires a reservation through the national park's online system, and permits release well in advance and sell out. Luaus and Molokini snorkel charters also fill up during peak weeks. Decide on your single big-ticket experience at least a month before you go -- our Haleakala sunrise guide covers the permit mechanics, and the drive from Kihei up to the summit takes about two hours and 45 minutes, so plan your sleep accordingly.

12. Pack the boring things that Maui actually charges a premium for

Reef-safe sunscreen, rash guards, beach towels, a hard-sided cooler and travel mugs are all marked up at resort shops. You also want at least one long-sleeve layer for Haleakala and for the wind on the lanai at sunset. Our complete Maui packing list goes deeper, but if you have limited luggage, prioritize the rash guard, a hat with a chin strap and a refillable water bottle.

13. Understand what "oceanfront" really means in a listing

Some oceanfront buildings have their ground-floor patios right off the sand. Others -- including ours -- sit elevated, with a private west-facing lanai 35 feet long on the seventh floor. That changes the experience. From the lanai at unit 711 you look southwest across the AuAu Channel toward Lanai and Molokai, which means sunsets happen directly in front of you and, from November through May, humpback whales are visible in the channel below. 14. Budget for the extras nobody mentions

Parking at a paid beach or trailhead runs $5 to $20, and entry to state parks and the Haleakala summit is $30 per vehicle. Rentals sometimes charge a cleaning fee separately. Restaurant prices run high, and tip expectations on the island are standard. The practical move is to budget a flat $100 a day per person for meals and incidentals, in addition to your lodging and rental car, and then track your spending the first two days so you can adjust. If you want the full cost breakdown, our Maui vacation cost guide has line items.

15. Leave room for the parts you didn't plan

The best mornings on Maui are the ones where you walk down to the water before sunrise, see nothing on the schedule and stay. The best afternoons are the ones spent on the lanai with a book and a plate of poke. If you have booked a base with the right location and a good view, you will find that you need fewer formal activities than you thought -- most first-timers end up saying exactly that.
