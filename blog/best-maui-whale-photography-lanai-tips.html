Maui Whale Photography Tips: How to Photograph Humpbacks From a West Maui Lanai

November 26, 2026 Wildlife 9 min read

Every winter, the AuAu Channel fills with humpback whales, and a west-facing lanai on Maui's napili-honokowai coastline becomes one of the best wildlife viewing platforms in the Pacific. This guide covers the practical side of shooting them: which gear actually helps, how the light behaves across the day, where the whales tend to surface, and how to come home with more than a hundred photos of empty water.

Why a West Maui Lanai Beats a Boat

Most whale photography happens on a rocking catamaran with twenty other people crowding the rail. A seventh-floor lanai at a place like Unit 711 at Maui Sands Seaside changes the equation entirely. The elevation puts you above the glare line on the water, which means you can see a whale's back and dorsal fin rather than a flat white reflection. You also get a 35-foot private deck facing due west across the channel toward Lanai and Molokai, so you can leave a camera on a railing mount, walk away for coffee, and pick it up the moment a breach breaks the horizon.

The other advantage is patience. Whale photography rewards time spent watching, not time spent shooting. From a boat you get two or three hours; from a lanai you get the whole week. That means you learn the local rhythm -- where the pods tend to gather in the morning, which part of the channel the mothers and calves favour, how a spout looks different when it belongs to an adult male versus a calf. If you want broader context on the season itself, our complete guide to humpback whale watching in Maui covers the migration calendar in detail.

One caveat worth stating plainly: the whales are far away. Even from a seventh-floor lanai, a breaching humpback in the AuAu Channel is often a quarter mile out. Photographing whales from shore is a long-lens game, and expectations should match that. The images that work are often the ones showing scale -- ocean, horizon, whale, sky -- rather than a frame-filling portrait.

Gear That Actually Earns Its Weight

If you are flying to Maui, every piece of camera gear competes for suitcase space. Here is what is worth carrying and what is not.

Bring a 70-200mm or 100-400mm zoom. Those are the two most useful ranges for lanai shooting. A 70-200mm on a crop-sensor body gives you a 105-300mm equivalent, which handles near whales and pod activity. A 100-400mm gives more reach for distant breaches but is heavier to hand-hold for an hour. Either works; a 400mm fixed prime does not, because whales move unpredictably and you will miss frames fumbling to reframe.

Bring a monopod or a rail clamp, not a tripod. A tall tripod crowds a lanai and slows your swing. A monopod with a small ball head, or a clamp that grips the lanai railing, gives you stability without the bulk. If neither is available, a rolled beach towel under the lens works surprisingly well as a rest.

Pack polarising and ND filters. A circular polariser cuts the glare off the water and makes a dark whale back read against a lighter sea. An ND filter lets you slow the shutter for a long exposure with tropical clouds streaking overhead while you wait for a whale.

Bring more memory and battery than you think. Breach sequences are burst-heavy. A single successful breach can consume 30 to 50 frames. Two spare batteries per body is not excessive when you are shooting all day from a lanai with a camera sitting on the railing.

Skip the super-teleconverter. A 2x converter on a consumer zoom drops you to a slow maximum aperture and softens images, and you lose the autofocus speed you need most. If reach is a real concern, rent a longer zoom in Kahului rather than stacking glass you already own.

Camera Settings for Breaches, Blows, and Tails

Whale photography from a lanai is an action problem disguised as a landscape problem. The settings that work for a still seascape will fail you the moment a 40-ton animal leaves the water.

Start at 1/2000 second, f/6.3, ISO auto with a 6400 cap. That combination handles a breach in bright light and gives you a little latitude if the light drops. For blows and tail slaps, 1/1000 second is enough. For a mother and calf cruising slowly past, 1/500 second will do.

Use continuous autofocus with a wide or zone AF area. Single-point AF will not find a whale against an empty blue background. Give the camera a wider search area and let it acquire. On mirrorless bodies, subject detection set to animals is worth testing but does not reliably recognise a surfacing whale; treat it as a bonus, not a plan.

Shoot in RAW. The dynamic range between a sun-sparkled sea and a dark whale back is brutal. RAW gives you two stops of recovery in the highlights that JPEG does not.

Turn on the electronic level. It sounds trivial, but a lanai horizon line at a two-degree tilt will ruin more shots than a slightly soft focus will. The AuAu Channel horizon is close to perfectly flat and the eye notices.

Set a custom white balance or shoot auto and correct later. Tropical haze shifts colour throughout the day. Auto white balance plus RAW is fine; a custom Kelvin reading of about 5500K gets you close most of the time.

Reading the Light Across the Day

A west-facing lanai is a light trap. It is spectacular in the late afternoon and almost unusable in the middle of a clear day.

Early morning, roughly 6:30 to 8:30, gives you side light from the east, which rakes across the water and puts texture into every ripple. Blows catch the light and glow. Breaches read against a still-dark sea with a bright sky behind them. This is the best window for dramatic whale images, and it happens while most visitors are still in bed.

Midday, roughly 10:00 to 15:00, puts the sun high and, from a west-facing lanai, behind you. The water flattens to a hard blue and the spray from a blow disappears into the background. This is the window for polariser work, long exposures, and shots that treat the channel as a landscape rather than a wildlife scene.

Late afternoon, roughly 16:30 to sunset, is the mirror of morning: warm side light, gold on the water, and the best conditions for silhouettes. A breaching whale against a Maui sunset is the cliche image for a reason, but it is genuinely hard to shoot because your exposure is dominated by the sky and the whale falls into shadow. Bracket and fix in post.

Overcast afternoons are underrated. Flat, even light removes the glare problem entirely and lets the whale's texture and the shape of a pectoral fin read clearly. Some of the most useful reference images of humpbacks are shot under cloud.

Timing and Whale Behaviour Patterns

Whale photography is largely the art of predicting where the animal will be two seconds from now. A few patterns are worth learning.

Watch the spout first. A humpback exhales before it dives, and a visible blow is your cue that the animal is at the surface and about to either dive or display. Train yourself to look for the puff, not the whale.

Breaches come in clusters. When one whale breaches, others nearby often follow within minutes. If you see a full breach, keep the camera up and stay on the general area for at least ten more minutes.

Tail slaps and pectoral fin waves are more common than full breaches and easier to anticipate. A whale that has been lobtailing repeatedly is likely to keep doing it. These make superb photographs because the splash is a strong compositional element.

Mothers and calves tend to stay closer to shore and move slowly, often within a few hundred yards of the reef. They are the best subjects for a long lens and for anyone learning the craft. They also breach, and a calf breach is disproportionately photogenic.

Early and mid season, roughly December through March, brings the highest concentration of surface-active adults. Late season, April and May, is quieter but offers calmer water and softer light. Framing the Shot Beyond the Whale

The mistake most first-time whale photographers make is cropping too tight. A humpback at 600 yards is not a portrait subject. The images that work are the ones that place the animal in its environment.

Include the horizon. A breaching whale with the Lanai coastline in the background tells a story that a whale against empty blue does not. A tenth of the frame given to the horizon line is usually enough.

Use foreground elements. The lanai railing, a palm frond, a coffee cup on the rail -- these give depth and place the viewer on the balcony. A whale seen past the corner of a lanai reads as a lived experience; a whale against bare water reads as a stock photo.

Shoot wide as well as long. Keep a second body or a phone with a wide lens nearby. A pod of whales with a rainbow over Molokai is a landscape photograph, not a wildlife one.

Leave headroom for the splash. When framing, place the whale's expected exit point in the lower third of the frame so the splash and spray have room above. Most breaches are lost to the top edge of the frame.

Bracket the sunset shots. Three-frame brackets at one-stop intervals give you a clean merge in post when the sky is bright and the whale is dark. If you want more ideas for the golden hour, our guide to the best sunset spots in Maui pairs well with lanai shooting.

Shooting With a Phone or Compact Camera

Not everyone travels with a long lens, and it would be dishonest to pretend a phone will produce a frame-filling breach shot. But a phone can produce genuinely good whale-season images if you shoot to its strengths.

Use the wide and ultra-wide lenses for context. A phone's strength is the scene: the lanai, the channel, the whales as distant spouts, the sky. That is a real photograph and often a more honest memory than a blurry crop of a distant whale.

Attach a small spotting scope or binoculars with a phone mount if you want reach without a camera. Digiscoping a whale at 400 yards through a stabilised spotting scope on a lanai railing clamp works, provided you lock focus and use the timer or a remote shutter.

Shoot 4K video and extract frames. A phone shooting 4K at 60fps gives you 60 chances per second to catch a breach at the moment of maximum extension. Pull a still from the peak frame and it will be usable on screen at moderate size.

Keep the phone out of the salt spray. A lanai rail is wet in the morning, and salt on a lens element is permanent damage in slow motion. A clear case and a microfiber cloth kept in a zip bag go a long way.

Marine Life Etiquette and Drone Rules

Humpbacks in Hawaiian waters are protected under federal law, and the rules apply to photographers on land as much as people on boats.

Keep distance from the water's edge. Approaching a whale that has come close to shore, or swimming toward one, is illegal and dangerous. From a lanai, distance is not an issue; from the shoreline, it is.

Do not fly a drone near a whale. Federal rules prohibit operating an aircraft, including a drone, within 1,000 feet of a humpback whale. That distance is measured vertically as well as horizontally, so a drone launched from a lanai to get closer is not permitted. If you are photographing from a seventh-floor lanai, you already have the elevation a drone would give you, and the whale is far enough out that you can enjoy it without breaking the law.

Watch for the honu. The reef below the property hosts Hawaiian green sea turtles, and they haul out on the shoreline at certain times of day. If you are already carrying a long lens for whales, a turtle on the reef is a fine subject while you wait for a spout. Our guide to where to see Hawaiian green sea turtles covers responsible viewing in more detail.

Keep it quiet and stay put. One of the quiet pleasures of a lanai is that you can observe a pod for an hour without disturbing it. That is the whole point.

Whale photography from a lanai is not really about the gear. It is about being in the right place for long enough that the animals forget you are there. A seventh-floor deck facing the AuAu Channel puts you in that place every morning of your stay. Frequently Asked Questions

What lens do I need to photograph humpback whales from a Maui lanai?

A 70-200mm or 100-400mm zoom covers most situations. Whales in the AuAu Channel typically breach between 200 and 800 yards offshore, so reach matters, but a super-long fixed prime can make it hard to track a moving animal. A zoom with fast autofocus and stabilisation is the practical choice.

What is the best time of day for whale photography from a west-facing lanai?

The first two hours after sunrise and the two hours before sunset give the most directional, low-angle light. Midday on a clear day puts the sun high and behind you when facing west, which flattens the water and washes out the spray from a blow. Overcast afternoons are often better than bright ones.

Which months are best for humpback whale photos in West Maui?

The season runs roughly November through May, with peak activity in January, February, and March. Late December through early April offers the highest odds of surface displays like breaches, tail slaps, and pectoral fin waving.

Do I need a tripod on a lanai?

A monopod or a beanbag on the lanai railing is usually more useful than a tripod. Whales move unpredictably across a wide arc, and a fixed tripod head is slow to swing. Hand-holding with a fast shutter speed and image stabilisation works well in daylight.

What shutter speed freezes a breaching humpback?

Start at 1/2000 second or faster and open up from there. A full breach lasts about a second, so burst mode plus continuous autofocus matters as much as shutter speed. For tail slaps and blows, 1/1000 second is generally sufficient.
